# Generate the meshes, keep the skeleton yours

The workable answer in October 2026 is a hybrid: use AI at authoring time to make **real meshes for each part separately** (a horse body, a human torso, a sword), then fit them to **one hand-made template skeleton per body plan** that the game owns, and keep the existing deterministic 8-facing baker, team mask and checks as the last step. None of the three tool families does the whole job. Text- and image-to-3D generators now give good surfaces fast and cheaply, which fixes the "lazy spheres" problem (C), but they return one fused object whose cut lines will never match the game's sockets. Auto-riggers handle quadrupeds only through one commercial API, and every source says they degrade on chunky, stylised or unusual bodies, which describes this whole roster. Direct 2D sprite generators make 8 facings of a single character but offer no swappable parts and no team-colour mask, so they cannot serve a game that builds races from prompts. The detached hands and spinning axes (B) are a symptom of rigid limbs on sockets; one skinned body per body plan, with gear parented to bones at a defined grip point, removes that class of bug rather than detecting it. Lumps (A) and missing hind legs need a layered automated check, and vision models are only good enough to be the last, recall-oriented layer, because they are weakest at exactly counting legs and seeing distorted anatomy. For a UK seller, Hunyuan3D's open weights are unlicensed outright; TRELLIS.2 (MIT) and paid Meshy or Tripo plans are the clean options, and any shipped AI-made art must be disclosed on Steam. The evidence on quality is mostly vendor-written, so the first step is a two-day experiment, not a commitment.

## Generators now make good surfaces but not socket-ready kits

The commercial front line has moved quickly and consolidated. Meshy lists `meshy-7.1` as its latest model ([Meshy API pricing](https://docs.meshy.ai/api/pricing)); Tripo's default is H3.1 from February 2026, with text, single-image and multiview inputs and PBR output ([Tripo developer docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/v3-1)); Rodin Gen-2.5 shipped in May 2026, though its specs come from resellers and press rather than an official page ([Scenario help](https://help.scenario.com/articles/3791851266-rodin-hyper3d-gen-2-5-models-the-essentials)). CSM, once the one service known for clean quad flow and separable parts, is gone: **Alphabet bought Common Sense Machines in January 2026 and csm.ai no longer resolves** ([StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/generative-3d-tools-comparison-meshy-rodin-tripo-csm-2026)). The rough consensus across 2026 roundups is Tripo for fast iteration, Meshy for all-round stylised game assets and Rodin for detail ([techsy.io](https://techsy.io/en/blog/best-ai-game-asset-generators)), but every one of those roundups is vendor-written or vendor-adjacent, and no independent, measured benchmark across the three turned up. Treat rankings as opinion.

For this game the useful feature is control over density, not raw detail. Tripo is the only API with explicit topology knobs: **"smart low-poly" at 500 to 20,000 faces, quad output up to 150k faces, about 40 s untextured and 120 s textured** ([Tripo developer docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/v3-1)). Meshy instead sells Remesh as a separate 5-credit call ([Meshy API pricing](https://docs.meshy.ai/api/pricing)). Since units are baked to small sprites, a few thousand faces per part is plenty; topology matters only where a part deforms (legs, torso) or must meet a socket cleanly. The best open model a UK developer can legally use is Microsoft's **TRELLIS.2 (4B, December 2025, MIT for code and weights)**, which writes PBR GLB and has a separate pipeline for texturing an existing mesh ([TRELLIS.2 GitHub](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2)). Its default export is a million faces with 4K textures, so a decimation pass is mandatory.

### Part separation exists only as after-the-fact segmentation

Commercial "parts" are segmentation of one generated object, not a kit. Tripo's `generate_parts` costs 20 extra credits but **only works untextured** ([Tripo developer docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/v3-1)); Meshy's "Auto Split" sits under its 3D-printing tools ([Meshy API pricing](https://docs.meshy.ai/api/pricing)). Research models do generate parts natively: PartCrafter makes several part meshes from one image ([arXiv 2506.05573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05573v1)), OmniPart plans part boxes and synthesises them with a part-aware TRELLIS ([arXiv 2507.06165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06165)), and Tencent's P3-SAM plus X-Part segments and completes parts of any mesh ([Hunyuan3D-Part GitHub](https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/Hunyuan3D-Part)). The licences rule several out: NVIDIA's PartPacker is non-commercial ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/PartPacker)), OmniPart mixes MIT with an NVIDIA source licence ([OmniPart NOTICE](https://huggingface.co/spaces/omnipart/OmniPart/blob/main/NOTICE)), and Hunyuan3D-Part probably carries Tencent's territory exclusion (unverified). Even where usable, a segmenter's cuts land where the model thinks parts are, not where `parts.json` puts its sockets. The reliable route for a parts kit is therefore to **generate each part on its own**, from its own concept image: a horse with no rider, a torso with no legs, a sword alone, a helmet alone.

### Centaurs and quadrupeds are where generators are weakest

No source tested any 3D generator on centaurs or hybrid anatomy. What evidence exists points the wrong way: image generators repeatedly fail to draw a centaur with a human torso and four horse legs ([Underground Designs](https://undergrounddesigns.substack.com/p/why-cant-chatgpt-draw-a-centaur)), generated horses often come with **legs welded together** ([Neural4D](https://blog.neural4d.com/image-to-3d/3d-horse-model-generator/), a vendor), and animal reconstruction research reports left/right leg confusion from side views ([3D Fauna](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.02400)). In an image-first pipeline the concept image is the bottleneck: image-to-3D faithfully reproduces whatever anatomy the picture got wrong. The game's own architecture is the fix here, because it already composes a centaur from a horse body and a human torso on sockets. Generating the halves separately sidesteps the hybrid problem, and the centaur's missing hind legs become a fitting and checking problem on a known horse mesh rather than a generation gamble.

### Style consistency is imposed downstream, not generated

No 3D generator offers a true style LoRA. Consistency comes from the input images (Tripo and Meshy take multiview input at the single-image price, and Tripo exposes seeds) and from retexturing afterwards ([Tripo developer docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/v3-1), [Meshy API pricing](https://docs.meshy.ai/api/pricing)). Vendors themselves list cross-asset style as an open problem ([3D AI Studio](https://3daistudio.com/3d-generator-ai-comparison-alternatives-guide/can-i-use-ai-for-indie-game-assets)). This game has an unusual advantage: it already owns its look at bake time, with fixed material colours, family cloth colours and a team-mask shader. The cleanest move is to **generate untextured geometry (cheaper: 20 credits on either API) and apply the game's own materials when baking**, which turns style consistency into a question of proportions and scale, which the sockets and anchors already control.

## Template skeletons beat learned riggers for a known roster

Auto-rigging is the step where the evidence is weakest and the risk is highest. Tripo is the only turnkey scripted option that covers non-humanoids: **rig types biped, quadruped, hexapod, octopod, avian, serpentine and aquatic, about 30 s and 25 credits per model, with a free "Rig Check" that recommends a type** ([Tripo rig docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/rig)). Its non-biped skeleton naming is not documented, which matters if one animation set must drive many creatures. Meshy's rigging covers humanoids, a "Quadruped Dog" and a beta "Smart Rig" that gets no animations, and its own docs list **"poor limb deformation" when proportions differ greatly from a standard human** ([Meshy rigging guide](https://docs.meshy.ai/en/webapp/guides/3d-model/rigging)). Mixamo is biped only with no API ([Tripo blog quoting Adobe](https://www.tripo3d.ai/blog/mixamo-alternative-that-can-rig-non-humanoid-or-quadruped), a competitor source). Independent testers report Tripo H3.1 auto-rigs "consistently fail at the joints" ([Neural4D](https://blog.neural4d.com/comparisons/tripo-3-1-model-review/), also a competitor), and a June 2026 vendor blog says auto-rigging "falls apart on anything unusual", with four-legged creatures often needing hand rigging ([Summer Engine](https://www.summerengine.com/blog/ai-3d-game-asset-generator)). Heroic 5.5-head humans, rats, frogs and bears sit squarely in that failure zone.

The open research riggers are real and permissively licensed: UniRig (MIT, SIGGRAPH 2025) predicts a skeleton then skin weights ([UniRig GitHub](https://github.com/VAST-AI-Research/UniRig)), its successor SkinTokens claims large skinning gains (authors' own numbers) ([arXiv 2602.04805](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2602.04805)), and Puppeteer (Apache-2.0) adds a video-guided animation optimiser ([Puppeteer GitHub](https://github.com/Seed3D/Puppeteer)). All are documented for CUDA only, with extensions such as `spconv` and `flash_attn` that make a Mac port non-trivial, and none was found running on Apple Silicon. More importantly, they predict a fresh skeleton per mesh, which makes animation reuse across units harder; UniRig's authors warn that a missing tail or wing bone degrades the skinning ([UniRig GitHub](https://github.com/VAST-AI-Research/UniRig)).

The roster here is small and known: humanoids of a few builds, horse, elephant, bear, rat, frog, centaur. That favours **one hand-built armature per body plan, fitted to each generated mesh**, then skinned in Blender. Blender's automatic weights fail on intersecting geometry, flipped normals, tiny scale or very dense meshes, with standard workarounds (merge, fix normals, decimate, scale up) ([BlenderArtists](https://blenderartists.org/t/bone-heat-weighting-failed/1240324)); decimated generator output is the easy case. Auto-Rig Pro ships horse and dog presets but no documented headless API ([BlenderNation](https://www.blendernation.com/2023/04/30/auto-rig-pro-rig-library-24-rig-presets-for-all-animals-big-and-small/)), so a plain `bpy` armature is the scriptable choice. A centaur is then a quadruped lower skeleton with a humanoid spine grafted at the withers: no tool lists a centaur rig type, so this is inference from standard layered animation, but it is ordinary rigging work.

### Animation stays procedural and keyed, as it is now

Preset libraries are thin for animals: Tripo has 90+ biped presets but only **16 "universal" presets for its v2.5/v3.0 non-biped rigs**, at 10 credits each ([Tripo animation docs](https://developers.tripo3d.com/en/models/animation)); Meshy gives quadrupeds a single walk ([Meshy rigging guide](https://docs.meshy.ai/en/webapp/guides/3d-model/rigging)). Animal text-to-motion research exists, such as AniMo with 78,149 sequences across 114 species ([CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/html/Wang_AniMo_Species-Aware_Model_for_Text-Driven_Animal_Motion_Generation_CVPR_2025_paper.html)), but it is research code tied to its own skeletons. An RTS needs perhaps five short cycles per body plan, and the game already keys legs per step (D-179). A four-beat walk or a diagonal-pair trot is a phase table per leg plus foot placement, which drives a template skeleton directly. Procedural Blender tools such as the free Spider Walker show the same idea baked to Actions ([Digital Production](https://digitalproduction.com/2026/08/28/spider-walker-automates-multi-leg-animation/)). ML adds little here.

### Skinned bodies with gear on bones end the detached-hand bugs

Game practice is consistent: **deforming gear (body armour, cloth) is skinned to the same skeleton as the body; rigid gear (weapons, shields, helmets) is parented to a bone with an offset** ([Babylon.js forum](https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/clothing-armour-adding-visual-armour-to-3d-animated-characters/2143), [Unreal Answers](https://answers.unrealengine.com/questions/855600/view.html)). Weapons often hang off dedicated bones under the hands so they can be swapped ([Unreal forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/unicornway-stylized-modular-goblin-rogue/2841995)), and Epic's weight-inpainting method transfers body weights onto armour of a different shape ([Epic, SIGGRAPH Asia 2023](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~rinat/projects/RobustSkinWeightsTransfer/preprint.pdf)). Applied here, a hand is part of a skinned arm, so it cannot detach; a weapon carries its own **grip point and grip axis** in data and is parented to a hand bone at that point, so an axe rotates about the palm instead of its own centre. Because sprites are baked offline, copying the nearest body weights onto close-fitting plate costs nothing at runtime. This is the single biggest structural fix for problem B, and it is independent of whether the meshes come from AI.

## Direct 2D sprite generators cannot carry a recipe-built roster

Three hosted tools claim 8 facings plus animation. PixelLab's Pro 8-rotation tool outputs a 3x3 grid of facings, **at most 168x168 px per frame**, with a quadruped body type and a "rotate character" mode, and makes no consistency claim in its docs ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/create-8-rotations-pro)). Ludo's directional sprites render up to 8 facings **from a single 3D model inside the tool** ([Ludo comparison](https://ludo.ai/compare/best-ai-sprite-generators)), which is telling: the vendor gets facing consistency through 3D, not pure 2D diffusion. A character-upload tool offering about 100 actions including 8-direction isometric walks exists too, with conflicting pricing ([aisotools listing](https://aisotools.com/tool/god-mode-ai)). Scenario, Leonardo and Layer are general platforms relying on trained models; Leonardo's output is not on a pixel grid and has no sprite animation ([Cinevva, Sept 2026](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/ai-pixel-art-generators)).

All of them work on whole characters. **No tool offers layered output with sockets, and no tool produces a team-colour mask.** The game builds races live from prompts across roughly five roles, many creatures and four ages; generating every combination whole is impossible, and the generated art could not be recomposed. The open multi-view models (MV-Adapter, Zero123++, Qwen Image Edit's multi-angle LoRA, Flux Kontext turnarounds) give four to six views, usually at eye level, with documented blur and lost detail in some views ([GeoMVD, arXiv 2511.12204](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.12204)); they are concept and training-data tools, not 8-facing isometric sprite makers.

The hybrid of baking 3D then repainting each frame with depth ControlNet holds silhouettes well ([Scenario help](https://help.scenario.com/comparing)) but flickers because each frame is denoised on its own; TemporalNet and low denoise reduce flicker and can shift colours ([MMagic docs](https://mmagic.readthedocs.io/en/stable/model_zoo/controlnet_animation.html)), and AI edits tend to break palettes ([pixie.haus](https://origin.pixie.haus/resources/edit-iterate-engineering-variations-with-image-to-image-ai), a vendor). Per-frame repainting would also drift the painted pixels away from the mask and the part boundaries the composer depends on. A vendor guide puts realistic character consistency at **about 85%, with hand cleanup** ([Lovart guide](https://www.lovart.ai/ja/blog/complete-guide-consistent-ai-character-design)), and one developer who swapped 3D renders for per-character LoRAs said continuity improved but it was not less work than the 3D renders ([itch.io devlog comment](https://itch.io/post/9221790), roughly 2024). The steadiest place for 2D AI in this pipeline is upstream: concept images that feed image-to-3D, and textures painted once per part, never per frame.

## Running it costs little; the licence and the label cost more

### Local Apple Silicon is viable for TRELLIS.2; rigging ML is not

TRELLIS.2 officially needs Linux and a 24 GB NVIDIA GPU, but the community trellis-mac port runs on PyTorch MPS at **about 3.5 minutes per mesh on a 24 GB M4 Pro, peaking near 18 GB, after a 15 GB weight download** ([trellis-mac wiki](https://github.com/shivampkumar/trellis-mac/wiki)); native MLX ports are early, one still missing its mesh decoder ([lyonsno/trellis2mlx](https://github.com/lyonsno/trellis2mlx)). That is fine for overnight batches of a few hundred parts. One trap: the official TRELLIS.2 pipeline calls RMBG-2.0 for background removal, which is CC BY-NC, so supply pre-cut images ([3daistudio](https://www.3daistudio.com/blog/is-trellis-2-free-pricing-how-to-get-started), secondary). Concept images can be made locally with FLUX.2 [klein] 4B, which is Apache 2.0, while the 9B variant is non-commercial ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/black-forest-labs/FLUX.2-klein-4B)); ComfyUI on MPS runs 2 to 4 times slower than CUDA and wants `--force-fp16` ([ComfyUI hardware support](https://mintlify.com/Comfy-Org/ComfyUI/configuration/hardware-support)). All Blender work (armatures, weights, keyed cycles, baking) runs locally. The ML riggers and cloud rigging do not, so rigging is either Blender with template skeletons or Tripo's API.

### Steam disclosure: required, and art draws the fire

Valve labels rather than bans. Its 2024 Content Survey splits **pre-generated** content (made with AI during development) from **live-generated** content ([Steam announcement](https://store.steampowered.com/news/group/4145017/view/3862463747997849618)), and a January 2026 revision reportedly narrowed disclosure to what players see or hear while exempting code assistants and pipeline tools ([tbreak](https://tbreak.com/steam-ai-disclosure-policy-update-developers/), secondary; the Steamworks form itself was not fetched). AI-generated meshes or textures that ship are pre-generated content and must be disclosed; the deterministic, rule-based race generator is non-AI procedural generation and is exempt, until an on-device prompt expander ships. Even the current code-built models were written with Claude's help; the conservative reading is to disclose plainly and specifically, which also suits the project's preference for honest public copy. Counts of labelled games vary (over 7,300 by March 2026 per [StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/steam-ai-disclosure-rules-2026-indie-developer-guide); more than 15,000 per SteamDB as cited by [GameFromScratch](https://gamefromscratch.com/steam-makes-massive-update-to-ai-generation-policy/)). The commercial risk is real but observational: disclosing games got **about 53% fewer reviews** than comparable ones in a 2025 cohort ([TechPowerUp](https://www.techpowerup.com/350230/games-made-with-gen-ai-suffer-up-to-53-worse-sales-on-steam)), and on games known to use AI, **78.9% of reviews mentioning AI were negative, with visual art the top complaint at 49.1%** ([GameDevAIHub](https://gamedevaihub.com/will-players-punish-ai-art-in-games/)). A large open-world action game replaced all its AI 2D props after player criticism in March 2026 ([Gigazine](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260323-crimson-desert-ai-art)). The backlash targets art that looks AI-made; a hand-directed, deterministic bake in the game's own palette is the strongest defence.

## Automated checks must layer geometry before vision models

The existing placement checks catch what they measure; "looks wrong" escapes because nothing measures it. Vision models are tempting judges but are weakest on this project's exact faults. In FineGRAIN, **anatomy accuracy across generators ranged from about 7% to 53%, and VLM judges often overlooked distorted anatomy or rationalised it as an optical illusion** ([arXiv 2512.02161](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02161v1)); VLMs struggle with compositional counting ([VLMCountBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04401)) and with fine counts such as animal legs ([arXiv 2509.08266](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.08266)); they lean "yes" on yes/no questions ([Arena-T2I Hard](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.31711)); and GPTEval3D, the VLM 3D judge, reaches only about **0.63 pairwise agreement with humans** per a competing benchmark ([Hi3DEval](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/42ffaddcc6edc9fb05ff9f9b49fca700-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)). A specialised detector localised body distortions better than VLMs in Distortion-5K ([arXiv 2503.00811](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.00811v1)). Asked whether a centaur has four legs, a VLM's prior answers for it. There are no published numbers for Claude specifically as a render judge, nor any benchmark on stylised sprites.

So the gate should be layered, cheapest and most deterministic first, with every bug a human catches turned into a fixture as the project already does. First, structural checks on the part graph and skeleton, which know the expected answer from data: every part touches its socket parent within tolerance, and the number of leg chains in the skeleton equals the legs declared in `parts.json` (the centaur check). Second, mesh checks on the posed, merged mesh: connected components via `trimesh.graph.split` (a floating hand is a second component) ([trimesh.graph](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.graph.html)); skeletonisation with skeletor to count end-points below the torso that reach the ground ([skeletor](https://github.com/navis-org/skeletor)), noting that medial skeletons can form false cycles when legs sit close together ([arXiv 2007.03483](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.03483)); a protrusion test for lumps (vertices standing out from a local smooth hull, or a sub-volume mostly outside its parent that is not a declared limb); and mirror symmetry by Chamfer distance outside declared asymmetric parts. These detectors are inference: no ready-made limb counter or published accuracy for stylised characters exists, and thresholds will need tuning per body. Third, 2D checks per facing: alpha islands in the sprite, ground-contact columns, silhouette IoU against the last approved bake, and DreamSim distance to approved art, which agrees with human similarity judgements about **96% of the time on its own dataset** ([DreamSim GitHub](https://github.com/ssnl/dreamsim)) but measures similarity, not correctness, and is untested on tiny sprites. Fourth, a VLM pass on diagnostic renders rather than final sprites: a large unshaded render, a flat per-part ID colour render and an 8-facing grid, asked open questions ("list each leg you see and where it meets the ground") before it is told what to expect, as dependency-ordered binary checks of the DSG kind ([VisExMEM](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.16441v1)), and pairwise against the last approved bake, flagging only when repeated runs agree. Last, the golden files, so any visual change needs explicit approval.

## The pipeline to build, and the experiment to run first

The recommended pipeline keeps everything that already works (8-facing bakes, sockets, team masks, keyed legs, placement checks, golden files, determinism) and replaces only the source meshes and the way limbs are joined. At authoring time, a part list drives a script: for each body plan and gear piece, a concept image is made in one fixed style (side and three-quarter views, plain background, T- or A-pose for bodies) with FLUX.2 klein 4B locally or a paid image tool; image-to-3D runs through Tripo H3.1 smart low-poly or Meshy 7.1 on a paid plan, with TRELLIS.2 locally as the free, licence-clean alternative, untextured by default. A Blender script (extending `tools/blender/`) decimates to a few thousand faces, normalises scale and orientation to the part's anchors in `parts.json`, fits the body plan's template armature by snapping its joints to landmarks, weights it with automatic weights, cuts legs at the hips where the data asks, gives each weapon a grip point and axis, and exports `.glb` into `assets/models/`, which the game already loads (`src/model/glb.lobster`). Animation stays as keyed cycles per body plan, now posing a skinned body instead of rigid spheres. Worn armour copies nearest-body weights; weapons, shields and helmets ride hand and head bones. The baker applies the game's materials and team mask, and the layered checks run before anything is shown. Nothing AI-driven runs at play time, so races stay deterministic and all fixed art can be prebaked and shipped.

Modding gets simpler, not harder, because the contract becomes "a `.glb` on a named body-plan skeleton, or a rigid `.glb` with a grip point", which is how modders already work in Blender and what any generator or hand modeller can produce. The same fitting script and checks ship in `tools/mods/` so a mod's art fails the same gate as the game's. The code-built primitive models can stay as a fallback and as placeholders for mods without art.

The biggest unknown is not whether generators make nice meshes; vendors show that daily. It is whether **AI parts, fitted to this game's sockets and skeleton and baked at this game's sprite size, look clearly better than today's sprites, and at how many hours of hand fitting per part**. If a fitted horse bakes to roughly what the spheres already give at 2x, the whole programme is moot; if it takes a day per part, it does not scale to the roster. The smallest experiment that answers both takes two or three days and under $50. Generate three parts: a riderless horse body, a heroic human torso with arms (5.5 heads, matching the current humans) and a sword. Make one concept image each, then run each through two tools, Tripo H3.1 smart low-poly via the API on a paid month and TRELLIS.2 through trellis-mac locally (if the Mac has 24 GB or more), giving six meshes. Fit each by script to the existing horse and human anchors, a template quadruped and biped armature and the hand socket, with the sword parented at its grip; also send the horse once through Tripo's quadruped auto-rig to compare its weights with the template's. Combine horse and torso into a centaur as a third case. Bake 8 facings with the existing baker through one walk cycle and one attack, lay them out with `src/sheet.sh` beside the current sprites of the same units at game zoom, and run `placement_check` plus a first version of the leg-count and alpha-island checks. Log minutes of hand work per part. Dominic then picks blind between old and new at game size. The result is a yes or no on quality, a per-part time cost, a pick between the two generators, and the first fixtures for the new checks, with no change to the shipped game.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The game's problems are less "the meshes are ugly" than "the limbs are separate rigid objects and nothing checks the whole body", and the strongest fixes for B and the centaur legs (one skinned body per plan, gear on bones at a grip point, leg counts checked from the skeleton against data) are structural and needed whatever the meshes' source. AI generation then becomes a replaceable supplier of surfaces into a contract the game owns, which also makes it the modders' contract, so neither a vendor's churn (CSM vanished in a year) nor a licence surprise like Hunyuan's can break the pipeline.

The candid gap is that almost every quality claim here is vendor-written and none concerns stylised quadrupeds at sprite size; the useful evidence will come from the game's own sheet, not from more reading. Vision models will help most as a triage step on purpose-made diagnostic renders and as a way for Claude to see the work at all, but the numbers say they cannot yet be trusted to count legs, so the human eye stays the final gate, ideally a little less often.
